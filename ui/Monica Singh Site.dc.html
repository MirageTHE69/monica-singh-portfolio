About

Hosting is listening first, speaking second.

I've spent over ten years on stages across Gujarat, from sangeet nights in family farmhouses to award ceremonies for Cera Tiles and UPL. The job looks like talking. It's mostly reading a room — knowing when a family wants the floor, when a CEO needs the clock respected, and when the best thing an anchor can do is step back.

Weddings are personal work. I meet the families before the function, learn the names and the in-jokes, and build the flow around the people who matter that night rather than a template script. Corporate events are the same instinct applied to a run sheet: brand names pronounced correctly, awards handed out cleanly, and a stage that never goes quiet.

Outside the mic I'm a fitness regular, an unrepentant food person, and an equalist — that last one shows up on stage whether or not anyone asks. I don't host jokes at anyone's expense. Warmth carries a room further than punchlines.

I host in Hindi, English and Gujarati, switching between them as the room needs rather than sticking to a fixed script. Ahmedabad is home; I travel across Gujarat as standard and pan-India for destination weddings and corporate events.

Sangeet anchor in Ahmedabad — Monica Singh

The sangeet is the night your family has been rehearsing for. I keep it moving — performances on cue, everyone introduced properly, and the energy in the room held from the first act to the last.

Why a sangeet needs its own host

A sangeet is the hardest function of the week to run. There are eight to fifteen performances, half of them rehearsed the night before, a DJ working from a different list, and two families who both want the floor. Without someone holding the order, the night runs an hour long and the last acts perform to a half-empty room.

A dedicated anchor fixes that. I take the performance list before the day, sequence it so the energy builds instead of sagging, and keep every changeover short enough that nobody sits down. When something slips — a costume change, a late uncle, a dance that doubles in length — the room never finds out.

How I host

Warm, confident, and never at anyone's expense. I learn the names before I arrive — the cousins, the chachas, the couple's story — so introductions sound like they come from inside the family rather than off a script.

I read the room continuously. When the grandparents are enjoying a slow number I let it breathe. When the floor is ready to open, I open it. And when the family wants the mic, I hand it over and step back, which is a skill more anchors should practise.
